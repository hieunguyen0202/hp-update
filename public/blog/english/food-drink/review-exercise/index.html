    Transcript — Extreme sports — experiences

    AHave you ever been bungee jumping?
    BNo, I haven’t. I’ve been skydiving, though.
    AWhat about you? Have you ever done any extreme sports like that?
    BDoes windsurfing count? I’ve tried windsurfing, although that was a long time ago.
    AI think windsurfing definitely counts! I’ve seen people doing it and they were going at crazy speeds. I’ve never done anything like that myself. Skydiving sounds very extreme to me.
    BWhere did you do it?
    AIt was in Spain. I did a tandem jump. It was fun, but I’m not sure I’d do it again.

    Talking about past experiences — present perfect

    Use the present perfect to talk or ask about experiences — only when you don’t say a time.

    • I’ve tried windsurfing.
    • I’ve never been bungee jumping.
    • Have you ever been skydiving?

    Talking about past experiences — more examples

    • I’ve read ‘The Idiot’.
    • I’ve never drunk whisky.
    • Have you ever grown your own vegetables?

    Food practice — Unusual food — present perfect

    AHave you ever tried oysters?
    BNo, I haven’t. I’ve tried lobster, though.
    AWhat about you? Have you ever eaten any unusual food like that?
    BDoes durian count? I’ve tried durian, although that was a long time ago.
    AI think durian definitely counts! I’ve never eaten anything like that myself. Blue cheese sounds pretty extreme to me.
    BHave you ever cooked something totally new?
    AI’ve made homemade pasta a few times. It’s harder than it looks!

    Talking about past experiences — present perfect · food

    Same grammar — life experiences, no specific time. Swap in food vocab.

    • I’ve tried sushi.
    • I’ve never been to a barbecue.
    • Have you ever grown your own herbs?
    • I’ve never tried sake.

    Talking about past experiences — past simple + time

    As soon as you mention a time, switch to the past simple.

    • I went windsurfing three years ago.
    • I didn’t drink a lot last year.
    • Did you eat a lot of sushi when you were in Japan?

    Present perfect → past simple

    "Have you ever been to Australia?"

    "Yes, I went there two years ago, for my friend's wedding."

    The question is present perfect, because it’s asking about experiences without mentioning a time. The answer mentions a time—two years ago—and so you need the past simple.

    Food practice — Food trip — present perfect → past simple

    AHave you ever been to Japan?
    BYes — I went there two years ago. I ate sushi almost every day when I was there.
    ANice! Did you try sake?
    BI didn’t drink a lot last year — but I did try ramen in Osaka last summer.

    Talking about past experiences — past simple + time · food

    Mention a time → switch to past simple.

    • I tried Korean BBQ last month.
    • I didn’t eat much fast food last year.
    • Did you eat a lot of sushi when you were in Japan?

    "Have you ever tried pho?"

    "Yes, I had it three years ago in Hanoi — it melted in my mouth."

    Transcript — Comparing past and present — used to dialogue

    AWow! You used to have a beard? You look so different!
    BYeah! That was during my punk rock phase.
    AReally? Did you use to be in a band or something?
    BYes, but it wasn’t anything big.
    BThere were a few of us who were all mates, and we would play in pubs or small clubs.
    ASo, what were you? Singer? Guitar?
    BDrummer! I used to play the drums.
    AAnd now? You don’t play any more?
    BNo, I gave up.

    Comparing past and present — used to

    Something was true in the past, but isn’t true now.

    • He used to have a beard.
    • I used to live in Berlin.

    Comparing past and present — didn’t use to

    Things that weren’t true in the past, but are true now.

    • They didn’t use to get on so well.
    • I didn’t use to wear glasses.

    Comparing past and present — questions

    • Did you use to play a musical instrument?
    • Didn’t he use to work here?

    would — past habits

    Actions or habits you did in the past, but don’t do now.

    • When we got home, Mum would make us beans on toast and then we’d watch cartoons.
    • There was this bakery near the office where I would go every lunchtime to get a sandwich and chat to the other regulars.

    any more

    Present verb + any more — similar meaning to used to.

    • She doesn’t live here any more.
    • I don’t have time to listen to music any more.

    Food practice — Childhood food — used to / would / any more

    AWow! You used to hate vegetables? You look so health-conscious now!
    BYeah! That was during my fast food phase.
    AReally? Did you use to eat junk food every day?
    BWhen I was a child, Mum would make us pho, and we would eat ice cream after school.
    AAnd now? You don’t eat fast food any more?
    BI don’t have junk food any more. I’d rather have a vegetable salad or fruit salad.

    Comparing past and present · food

    Same structures — childhood eating habits vs now.

    • I used to hate vegetables — now I love a good vegetable salad.
    • They didn’t use to get on with spicy food — now they order curry every week.
    • When we got home, Mum would make us pho and then we’d watch cartoons.
    • I don’t eat processed food any more.

    Transcript — Telling a story — setting the scene

    AOh! Have I told you what happened to us on our trip?
    BNo! What happened?
    AIt’s a really crazy story.
    ASo, we were sitting on the bus, ready to leave…
    BWhere were you going?
    ASofia.
    AAnyway, the weather was awful. It was raining so hard you couldn’t even see out of the window, and…
    BWho were you travelling with?
    AWith my wife. We were planning to visit some old friends who…
    BWhere was the bus leaving from?
    AFrom Athens.
    ALook, can I tell my story, or not?
    BOh, sorry…

    Past continuous — set the scene

    Describe the background at the start — who was there and what was happening.

    • We were sitting on the bus, ready to leave.
    • It was raining so hard you couldn’t even see out of the window.
    • I was living in a small apartment at the time.
    • I was driving home after work.

    Useful for longer answers in a job interview or IELTS — not only long stories.

    Food practice — Kitchen story — past continuous

    AHave I told you what happened at the dinner party?
    BNo! What happened?
    ASo, we were sitting in the kitchen, ready to grill the ribs…
    BWho were you cooking with?
    AWith my sister. I was chopping garlic and ginger, and she was marinating the meat, and…
    BAnd then?
    AThe weather was awful. It was raining so hard we couldn’t even use the barbecue outside.

    Past continuous — set the scene · food

    • I was chopping vegetables when the guests arrived.
    • We were grilling kebab in the garden.
    • I was living on instant noodles at the time.

    Transcript — Driving test story

    ADid I tell you about my driving test?
    BNo, what happened?
    AI passed!
    AYou know, I took it last week, and I hadn’t taken any lessons. Not one!
    BNo way! But, you must have practiced at least?
    ANo! I had only driven a car twice in my life
    BHow on earth did you pass?
    AIt was rush hour. We drove out of the test centre, and then we sat in a traffic jam. All of the streets were totally stuck. I made three left turns, and finally we arrived back at the test centre. I just drove around the block once!
    BAnd that counts as a pass?
    AHey, I didn’t make any mistakes.
    BSo what happened next? Did you drive home?
    AWell…
    BWhat happened?
    AI tried, and I had a very small accident. I mean, I don’t think it even counts as an accident.
    BMaybe you should take some driving lessons.
    AVery funny.

    Time reference — fix when the story starts

    • Last week…
    • This happened two years ago, in summer.
    • So, yesterday, I was walking down the street…

    A time reference ‘fixes’ when your story starts — then you can talk about events before and after that point.

    Past perfect — before the story starts

    Use had + past participle for things that happened before the story’s starting point.

    • I hadn’t taken any driving lessons.
    • I had only driven a car twice in my life.
    • When I was 25, I quit my job and decided to train as a pilot. I had always wanted to learn to fly.

    Past simple — events in the story

    For what happens during the story (after the starting point), use the past simple.

    • We drove out of the test centre.
    • We sat in a traffic jam for ages.
    • I had a small accident on the way home.

    Food practice — Dinner disaster — past perfect vs past simple

    ADid I tell you about last week’s dinner?
    BNo, what happened?
    AI invited friends last Saturday, and I hadn’t marinated the ribs the night before. Not once!
    BNo way! But you must have cooked something?
    AI’d never roasted a whole chicken before that night. We sat in the kitchen for ages. I made three side dishes, and finally we grilled everything outside instead.
    BAnd that counted as a dinner party?
    AHey, I didn’t burn anything. Well… I had a very small accident with the frying pan.

    Past perfect vs past simple · food

    Time reference fixes the story start. had + PP = before that; past simple = events in the story.

    • I had never tasted blue cheese before that trip.
    • I hadn’t marinated the meat the night before.
    • We grilled the ribs outside instead.

    Transcript — Regrets — languages

    ADo you speak any other languages?
    BNot really. I used to speak Spanish, but I haven’t used it for years.
    BI wish I’d started learning other languages when I was younger. It’s so much easier if you start earlier.
    AYeah, I know what you mean. If only I’d kept my Spanish going…
    AWhy don’t you pick it up again? It’d come back.
    BMaybe…
    BYou know what, though? I wish I’d spent some time in Latin America when I had the chance.
    BI could have lived there for a year or two, and my Spanish would have got really good.
    AWell, you could still do it, right?

    wish + past perfect

    Talk about something you regret — often the opposite of what really happened.

    • I wish I’d learned other languages when I was younger.
    • I wish I hadn’t said that.

    if only + past perfect

    • If only I’d kept my Spanish going.
    • If only I hadn’t wasted so much time.

    could have — regrets

    • I could have tried harder.
    • If I hadn’t left things to the last minute, I could have passed easily.

    Food practice — Food regrets — wish / if only / could have

    ADo you cook much at home?
    BNot really. I used to cook pasta every night, but I haven’t used the wok for years.
    BI wish I’d started eating plant-based meals when I was younger. It’s so much easier if you start earlier.
    AYeah. If only I’d kept my low-carb diet going…
    BI could have learned to make sushi when I had the chance. My nutrition would have got really good.

    wish · if only · could have · food

    • I wish I’d learned to cook curry when I was younger.
    • If only I hadn’t wasted so much money on takeout.
    • I could have tried harder with the gluten-free diet.

    Food: When I was a child, I used to hate vegetables, but now I love a good vegetable salad.

    Transcript — The wedding — what went wrong

    AHow was the wedding?
    BA disaster! I’ve never seen anything go so wrong.
    AWhy? What happened?
    BFirst, they had booked a hall for the ceremony, but it was much too small. Only 30 people could go in, and everyone else had to wait outside.
    AReally? That’s weird.
    AI know! Surely they knew how many people they had invited?
    BI guess not. Sounds bad.
    BYes, but that’s not all.
    BThey’d booked a restaurant for the reception, but they hadn’t told them how many people were coming. So, there wasn’t enough food, either!
    AThat’s not good.
    BAnd then, as if that wasn’t enough, there were so many long, boring speeches! You could tell that no one had prepared their speeches, and they were just trying to improvise. It just went on and on.
    ASo, you’re hungry and listening to boring speeches for hours? Doesn’t sound like much fun.
    BIt wasn’t.

    Past perfect — form

    • You need ‘had’ or ‘hadn’t’ plus a past participle. For example, ‘had gone’, ‘hadn’t prepared’, and so on.

    Past perfect examples from the wedding dialogue

    • they had booked a hall for the ceremony
    • They’d booked a restaurant for the reception
    • they hadn’t told them how many people were coming
    • no one had prepared their speeches

    Past perfect — form & meaning

    Form: had / hadn’t + past participle. Use when you need the past in the past — something before another past time.

    • Form — had + past participle — had booked · hadn’t prepared
    • Earlier past — They had booked a hall for the ceremony, but it was much too small.
    • Timeline — preparations (had…) → wedding day (past simple)

    Food practice — Wedding buffet — past perfect

    AHow was the wedding dinner?
    BA disaster! I’ve never seen a buffet go so wrong.
    AWhy? What happened?
    BFirst, they had booked a tiny hall, but they’d ordered lobster and oysters for 200 people. Only 30 could sit inside.
    BThey’d booked a restaurant for the reception, but they hadn’t told them how many people were coming. So there wasn’t enough seafood either!
    AAnd the cheesecake? Had anyone even marinated the meat?
    BYou could tell that no one had prepared the speeches — or the mustard and soy sauce for the table.

    Past perfect — form & meaning · food

    had / hadn’t + past participle — food events before the meal.

    • They had booked a restaurant, but it was much too small.
    • They hadn’t told the chef how many guests were coming.
    • I had never tasted blue cheese before that dinner.

    Transcript — When did you start teaching?

    AWhen did you start teaching?
    BActually, it was kind of an accident. It was 2005. I had just graduated, and I wasn’t sure what I wanted to do. So, I took a six-month teaching job, mostly because I wanted to live abroad and travel a bit.
    ASo, you didn’t want to teach?
    BNot really! I had never considered it as a career.
    AWhere did you move to?
    BRussia. I had studied a little bit of Russian at university, but not enough to really be able to do anything. So, I wanted to learn more, and also just experience living in Russia.
    AHad you ever lived abroad before?
    BBriefly. I’d spent some time in Canada, but this was more challenging.

    Past perfect in conversation

    • I had just graduated, and I wasn’t sure what I wanted to do.
    • I had never considered it as a career.
    • I had studied a little bit of Russian at university, but not enough to really be able to do anything.

    You use it when you’re already talking about the past, and you want to refer to something which is *further* in the past.

    Food practice — First cooking job — past in the past

    AWhen did you start cooking professionally?
    BIt was 2018. I had just graduated, and I wasn’t sure what I wanted to do. So I took a six-month job in a kebab shop.
    ASo you didn’t want to be a chef?
    BNot really! I had never considered it as a career. I had studied a little nutrition at university, but not enough to run a kitchen.
    AHad you ever lived abroad before?
    BBriefly. I’d spent some time in Thailand — that’s where I first tried curry and shellfish.

    Past perfect in conversation · food

    • I had just graduated, so I took a job making baguettes.
    • I had never considered plant-based cooking as a career.
    • I had studied a little nutrition, but not enough to be a chef.

    Transcript — Late for work — when NOT to use past perfect

    AWere you late for work *again*?
    BYeah… What happened?
    AMy alarm clock didn’t go off this morning.
    ASo what time did you get there?
    BAround eleven.
    AEleven?! Why did you wake up so late?
    BI couldn’t fall asleep last night. I probably got four hours of sleep.
    ADid you go to bed late?
    BNot really. I think it was around twelve.

    When past perfect is NOT needed

    • When I moved to the USA, I found a job.
    • When I moved to the USA, I had found a job.

    When the order that things happened is clear, you don’t need to use the past perfect.

    Past perfect vs past simple — meaning

    When the order of events is obvious, past simple is enough. Past perfect changes meaning when sequence matters:

    • When I moved to the USA, I found a job.
    • When I moved to the USA, I had found a job.

    First = moved then found job. Second = found job before moving.

    Past perfect vs past simple · food meaning

    Sequence changes the meaning:

    • When I moved to Italy, I found a job in a pasta kitchen. → moved first, then the job.
    • When I moved to Italy, I had found a job. → job first, then the move.

    Food: Before that dinner, I had never tasted blue cheese — so the flavour shocked me.

    Transcript — Intro — why stories matter

    GinaHi, I’m Gina. Welcome to Oxford Online English! In this lesson, you can learn how to tell a story in English.
    GinaStories are powerful. When you meet someone new, go to a job interview or take a speaking exam like IELTS, you need to tell stories, whether you realise that’s what you’re doing or not.

    Step 1 · Background

    Start with who · when · where · what. Add 1–2 detail sentences — don’t drag.

    • It was summer, and I went with some friends to a beach in Crimea which you could only get to by boat.
    • At university, I shared a flat with three other guys.

    AIt was last month, and I invited some friends over for ribs and a vegetable salad.
    BAt university, I shared a flat with three other guys who lived on instant noodles and donuts.

    Step 1 · Background · food

    who · when · where · what — for a meal story.

    Transcript — Story 1 — opening & background

    GinaIt was summer, and I went with some friends to a beach in Crimea which you could only get to by boat.
    GinaPeople had been going there for years, and there were benches and tables, places to camp, fire places and so on. It was kind of a hippy place, with everyone walking around naked and doing whatever they felt like.

    Step 2 · Goal

    What did the people in the story want?

    • On the last day, we had to catch a train in the evening.
    • To reach Issyk-Kul lake, which was the end of my journey, I had to cross a mountain pass, almost 4,000m high.

    Step 2 · Goal · food

    • We had to finish roasting the chicken before the guests arrived.
    • I had to cross the market to buy oysters before the stall closed.

    Transcript — Story 2 — tension (mountain pass)

    GinaOn the third day, I had to cross a mountain pass, almost 4,000m high. It was so hard, because the air is thin up there and I was carrying a very heavy pack.
    GinaIt seemed to take forever, but finally I got close to the top… and then a storm boiled over the ridge and landed right on my head. There was lightning all around me, even below me! The noise was unbelievable.

    Step 3 · Tension

    The goal must not be too easy. Listeners should wonder: what’s going to happen?

    • Problems or difficulties before the climax
    • Foreshadowing — e.g. I told them not to do anything to my room. I knew they were going to do something.

    Food practice — Meal story — tension

    AThe oven broke. The ribs weren’t ready, the guests were at the door, and we still had to chop the herbs.
    BI knew something would go wrong — I told them not to touch my cheesecake. I knew they were going to do something…

    Step 3 · Tension · food

    • Broken oven / missing ingredient / rain on the barbecue
    • Foreshadowing: I told them not to touch my cheesecake. I knew they were going to do something.

    Gina“Don’t worry, we won’t. Have a good weekend,” they said.

    Transcript — Endings — resolve tension

    GinaWe loaded our stuff onto a kayak and swam almost a kilometre around the cliffs. A naked hippy paddled the kayak, which was piled high with our things and looked like it could sink at any minute. We made it to land, and after several hours of hitchhiking and walking, we caught our train.
    GinaIt was stressful at the time, but looking back now it makes a good story!
    GinaThey made my room into a jungle! I’m not kidding: there were flowers, plants, three whole trees, jungle animals made from paper, and a ‘sounds of the forest’ mix playing on my stereo.

    Step 4 · Ending + retrospective comment

    • Resolve the tension — answer what happened
    • It was stressful at the time, but looking back now it makes a good story!
    • It was a very frightening experience.

    Food practice — Meal story — ending

    AWe grilled everything outside instead. Looking back, it was stressful at the time, but it makes a good story!
    BThey made my fridge into a dessert shop — cupcakes, pudding, even a popsicle tower. It took me three hours to clean up.

    Step 4 · Ending · food

    • Looking back, it was one of the best barbecues I’d ever shared with friends.
    • In the end, we grilled outside — and it tasted even better.

    Word stress — contrast & correction

    Adding word stress is a simple way to add emphasis to your idea. This is especially useful when you want to correct someone, or disagree with somebody else.

    Word stress — what it means

    Stressing one word shows contrast or corrects someone. Examples from the video:

    • Stress tomorrow → he is flying to Paris, but not tomorrow.
    • Stress flying → he is going to Paris tomorrow, but not by plane.
    • Stress he → other people are flying, but he isn’t.

4 · Core formulas — Lesson 2 & 3

Công thức IELTS Nguyễn Huyền — Lesson 3 (Yes/No + Reasons) ghép Lesson 2 (lý do thích / không thích). Chọn 1–2 nhánh, không nhồi hết.

Lesson 3 · Do you like X?

Công thức: Yes/No + Reasons (dùng Lesson 2)

Do you like X?
├── YES
│   ├── Yes, definitely / absolutely
│   ├── I + V (like / love / enjoy) + V-ing
│   ├── I'm + adj (interested in / keen on)
│   └── I'm a + NP (big fan of)
├── NO
│   ├── No, definitely / absolutely not · No, not really
│   ├── I + DON'T + V
│   ├── I'm + NOT + adj
│   └── I'm NOT a + NP
└── REASONS
    ├── Because / This is because + S + V
    └── Because of + noun / noun phrase
YesNo
DirectYes, definitely / absolutelyNo, definitely not · No, not really
VerbI like / love / enjoy + V-ingI don't like / love / enjoy
AdjI'm keen on / interested inI'm not keen on / interested in
NPI'm a big fan ofI'm not a big fan of
Reasonbecause / This is because + S + V · because of + noun/NP
  • It gives me the chance to + V
  • I also get the opportunity to + V
  • It's a great way to + V · It also helps me + V
  • can lead to various health problems, such as…

Lưu ý: because it is not good for my health (mệnh đề) ↔ because of its harmful effects on my health (cụm danh từ)

Thực hành · Food & general

Do you like cooking?

Yes, definitely. I'm keen on cooking because it gives me the chance to try new recipes and unwind after work.

Vâng, chắc chắn. Tôi thích nấu ăn vì nó cho tôi cơ hội thử món mới và thư giãn sau giờ làm.

Do you like fast food?

No, definitely not because it's not good for my health. Consuming too much fast food can lead to various health problems, such as diabetes, high blood pressure or even cancer.

Không, chắc chắn không vì không tốt cho sức khỏe. Ăn quá nhiều đồ ăn nhanh có thể dẫn đến tiểu đường, cao huyết áp hoặc ung thư.

Do you like trying new cuisines?

Yes, absolutely. I'm a big fan of food from different cultures. This is because it gives me the chance to explore different traditions and widen my horizons.

Có, tôi là fan của ẩm thực đa dạng — được khám phá truyền thống và mở rộng tầm nhìn.

Do you like playing sports? health

Yes, definitely, because it's a great way to keep fit and stay healthy. It also helps me strengthen my muscles and burn excess calories.

Vâng — giữ dáng, khỏe mạnh, tăng cơ và đốt calo.

Do you like music? V-ing subject

Yes, absolutely. I'm a big fan of music. This is because listening to music helps me relax, unwind and temporarily forget all the pressures from my work.

Nghe nhạc giúp thư giãn và tạm quên áp lực công việc. (listening to music = V-ing làm chủ ngữ)

Do you like your job? soft no

Well, not really because my job is quite boring. It doesn't give me the chance to try anything new. I have to deal with the same tasks every day.

Không thực sự thích — công việc nhàm, không có gì mới, lặp lại mỗi ngày.

Grammar notes (Lesson 3)
  • V-ing làm chủ ngữ: Listening to music helps me relax. · Watching movies is a great way to unwind.
  • -ing vs -ed: boring (tính chất) vs bored (cảm xúc) — This movie is boring · It makes me bored
  • every day (adv) vs everyday (adj)
